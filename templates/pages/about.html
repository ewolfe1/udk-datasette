A note on using the descriptions

Descriptive metadata, OCR text, object detection, and map placement for items in this collection has been generated in part using AI (artificial intelligence) technologies and may be incomplete, misleading, or inaccurate. Any metadata listed on this site or found in the data is not to be considered as ground truth and should always be verified against the original page.

About This Archive

The KU Student Newspaper Archive provides full text search and browse access to the student newspapers published at the University of Kansas from 1878 to 2017 — about 190,000 pages and two million individual items, including articles, advertisements, and the full text of every page.

The newspapers themselves are held by KU Libraries and can be explored chronologically as full page images in KU's Digital Collections at https://digital.lib.ku.edu/ku-udk/root. This archive presents highly detailed access to that collection. Rather than presenting the papers page by page, it divides them into individual, searchable items, making it possible to find and compare material across the full 140-year run.

Image source and known issues

These images have been digitized from the original microfilm reels, imaged at the Kansas State Historical Society beginning in the 1970s. As a result, there may be issues with image quality, duplicate or missing pages, legibility, or other concerns. Coverage depends on the contents of the microfilm, so some issues and pages are missing. In some instances, smaller pages were photographed on top of larger pages, resulting in considerable overlap of visible text and duplicated or inaccurate metadata for a given page.

AI and Automated Processing

Most of the textual data in this archive — the page text, the division of pages into items, and nearly all of the descriptive information — was produced using automated tools, including AI, machine learning, neural networks, and other methods. Although these tools are what allow the level of detail included in this collection, it also means the data contains errors and should always be verified against the actual images. Items and articles

A vision model, GLM-4.5V, read each page image and identified the separate content blocks on it — articles, photographs, weather forecasts, letters to the editor, and so on — then generated a title, descriptive summary, subjects, and named entities for each. (GLM-4.5V is a large, open-source vision model, about 106 billion parameters, developed by the lab Zhipu AI; it interprets images as well as text.)

The model did not record where each item appears on the page. When an item is highlighted on the page image, that location is an approximation, derived by matching the item's title against the page text. It is usually close, but may be incomplete or, occasionally, wrong.

Advertisements

Advertisements were identifed through the use of LayoutParser, a neural network trained on the Library of Congress' Newspaper Navigator model. This tool identifed the bounding box for each detected ad, which was subsequently analyzed by GLM-4.5V to generate metadata such as a category, keywords, advertiser's name, an address, and other details. Because ads have actual bounding boxes, their highlighted regions are generally more accurate than those for articles. LayoutParser's detection is imperfect - some ads are missed, particularly early ads set as plain text; adjacent ads are sometimes merged into one; or the box may drawn too large or too small.

Addresses detected through this process were cleaned and normalized, then geocoded with OpenStreetMap via Nominatim (with ArcGIS used as a backup search in some cases) to identify latitute and longitude coordinates. Due to a variety of complex issues with OCR detection, partial or imprecise addresses, and a variety of other factors, mapped locations may have errors.

Page text (OCR)

The searchable text was produced by GLM-OCR, an AI model that reads the scanned page images. Unlike traditional OCR, which recognizes letters one shape at a time, GLM-OCR reads in context, using the surrounding words to interpret smudged or unusual type. (It is also open-source and from Zhipu AI, but small enough to run on modest hardware.) This handles old and worn pages well, but it introduces a limitation traditional OCR does not have: the model can occasionally produce a word that fits the context but was not actually printed on the page.

In all cases, accuracy depends heavily on the original. Faded ink, damaged pages, decorative headlines, and ornate advertising type are the most error-prone. The text is reliable for searching and discovery, but the original page image should be used for quotation or citation.

Using This Archive

Search runs across articles, advertisements, and page text at once, and results can be filtered by date, source type, paper, category, subject, and named entity. The ? on the search page documents the supported syntax, including phrase searches, boolean operators, and proximity searches.

The site is built on Datasette, so the underlying data is also open: it can be browsed as tables, downloaded, or queried directly with SQL. This makes the archive usable not only for reading but as a dataset for research.

Citations generated by the viewer include the paper, date, volume, issue, and a permalink. Because the descriptions are AI-generated, confirm the title and page against the original image before citing.
